Darnell Taylor: Insanity Defense, Verdict and Open Questions

The Crime Vault 247 · November 19, 2026 · Case: Darnell Taylor

Darnell Taylor lived for five years and 26 days. He was not taken by a stranger or lost on a crowded street. He died in the home he had been placed in to be cared for. The question at the center of this case is not who, but why, and what the jury was asked to decide about the woman responsible: was she too ill to know right from wrong, or did she know exactly what she was doing?

Who Darnell was

Darnell was born on January 18, 2019, in Columbus, Ohio. His family describes a bubbly boy who was always smiling and always dancing, and who loved superheroes. When police searched for him, they released a description that included Spider-Man pajamas.

His biological grandparents had cared for him and later turned to a close family friend, Pammy Maye, because they did not want him in the system. Police said Darnell began living in Maye's home around May 2023. After July 2023, his family says, they never saw him again.

What happened

At 3:03 a.m. on February 14, 2024, Maye's husband called 911. He said his wife had woken him and told him Darnell was "not alive," and that she left in a gray Jeep Cherokee. According to a court affidavit, she tried to cover his mouth when he reached for the phone. He also told the dispatcher his wife had depression and bipolar disorder but had been taking medication. Police said he cooperated and has not been charged.

An Amber Alert was issued at 5 a.m., and a warrant was issued for kidnapping and child endangering. Nearly two days later, Maye walked into an envelope factory in Brooklyn, Ohio, disheveled, with no shoes, and asked workers to call her husband. Police arrested her. She said she understood her rights. She then told detectives where to find Darnell, and his body was found in a sewer drain in Columbus.

The investigation

According to prosecutors, the trigger was that Darnell had taken a granola bar and peanut butter crackers into his bed. They said that at 3 a.m. on February 13, Maye decided to end his life, and that the act came hours later, at 11:06. Prosecutors also said she switched her car's license plate, dressed in dark clothing and drove to look for a suitable storm sewer.

In a recorded conversation with police, Maye used the word "premeditated." Her attorney, Sam Shamansky, did not dispute her statements but said they did not determine her mental state at the time of the offense.

The trial and outcome

Maye was indicted on one count of aggravated murder, three counts of tampering with evidence and one count of gross abuse of a corpse, and held on a $4 million bond. In August 2025 a judge accepted a report finding her incompetent to stand trial, and she was sent for treatment. Later she was found competent. The defense then pursued a plea of not guilty by reason of insanity.

The video explains that these are different legal questions. Competence concerns whether someone can understand court proceedings and assist her lawyer. Insanity concerns whether she understood her act was wrong when it happened.

A psychologist testified that Maye has had bipolar disorder since 2008 but likely understood her actions were wrong. Jurors saw body camera video in which she called herself "selfish" and referred to "thou shalt not kill." Police commander David Hughes described her as callous. In closing, the defense asked whether she was "evil personified" or so sick she could not appreciate the wrongfulness of the act.

On November 10, 2026, the jury found Maye guilty on all five counts. Sentencing was expected in the weeks that followed, and she faces life in prison.

Questions that remain

Darnell had been known to the system since 2022. In March 2022, Franklin County Children Services substantiated an allegation of physical abuse or neglect, took emergency custody and placed him with his paternal grandparents. His grandmother says she raised concerns with his caseworker repeatedly. Police had been to the Maye home three times with medics on medical calls.

In November 2024, Darnell's estate filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Franklin County Children Services, Permanent Family Solutions and the county commissioners, claiming he was left exposed to long-standing harm. None of those claims has been proven in court.

The courtroom has answered who killed Darnell. Who failed to protect him remains open. Should mental illness change a sentence when someone knew what she was doing was wrong?
