Nola Dinkins: A False Amber Alert and Two Different Verdicts

The Crime Vault 247 · November 22, 2026 · Case: Nola Dinkins

When police in three states searched through the night for a 3-year-old girl allegedly taken by an armed stranger, where was she really, and why did no one see the danger before that night?

On June 10, 2025, a mother reported that her daughter, Nola Dinkins, had been taken from her car in Newark, Delaware. An Amber Alert went out in Delaware, Maryland and Pennsylvania, and the FBI joined the search. Less than 24 hours later, police canceled the alert. There was no kidnapper.

Who Nola Was

Nola's family called her "Nola Bear." Her paternal grandmother, Velma Dinkins, said of her: "She captured everybody's heart. From the moment you saw her, you fell in love with her." Her father described her as a child with an infectious energy who left a lasting impression on everyone she met. He was her one and only child's father, and he lived in Indiana.

Distance kept that side of the family from seeing her often. A custody dispute between her father and her mother, Darian Randall, had stretched on since 2023. During that dispute, Randall's own father wrote a letter to an Indiana court calling her a "very hardworking young lady and a really good mother to Nola." Court records also show that in March 2025, Randall withdrew her request to move to Maryland, and the parents agreed to parenting arrangements in Indiana. Nola was living in Maryland when she died.

What Happened

Randall told Newcastle County police that an unknown white man with a handgun had taken Nola, and that he fled in a dark SUV driven by a white woman. Officers searched near the location she described, including a nearby apartment complex.

Investigators soon turned toward Cecil County, Maryland, where Nola lived. According to court records reported by Fox 29, the man Randall lived with, Cedric Britton, said the child had left with her mother. Security cameras showed only Randall walking out alone. Police also noted a smell of bleach and gloves inside the home.

Detectives concluded that Randall's account was false, and the case became a homicide investigation led by the Maryland State Police. According to charging documents, Randall said she invented the kidnapping to deflect attention from her boyfriend. At 1:45 p.m. on June 11, Nola's remains were found in a suitcase on a private road behind Britton's home in Cecil County.

The Investigation

Randall was charged with first- and second-degree murder, first-degree child abuse resulting in the death of a minor, and related counts. Britton was charged as an accessory after the fact to murder, with failure to report a child's death, and with related counts. Both were presumed innocent until proven guilty in court.

Charging documents say Randall told police she struck Nola with a belt, and that Nola stopped breathing. They also say Britton tried CPR, and that he later showed police on a map where the suitcase had been left. Troopers also reported finding a baby blanket that appeared to have blood on it.

The physical evidence pointed to something longer than a single day. An expert testified that Nola lost more than 7 pounds in just over a month and weighed only 19 pounds when she died. An assistant medical examiner testified that she died of blunt force trauma and that the manner of death was homicide. A child abuse expert testified that Nola had suffered physical abuse, emotional abuse and neglect.

A digital examiner testified about Randall's phone. Prosecutors presented searches that included how to terminate parental rights, a boarding school for preschoolers, how to remove blood stains, and how to get away with murder. They also presented a text from Britton reading, in part, "having Nola has changed you for the worst."

The Trials and Outcomes

Randall's trial began with a jury pool of 120 people. Seventy-four said they held strong feelings about the charges. The defense argued that Randall was not competent to stand trial, but the judge disagreed and the trial moved forward. After six days of trial, the jury deliberated for less than two hours and found Randall guilty of first-degree murder and other charges. She faces a maximum penalty of life without parole and is scheduled to be sentenced on February 5, 2027.

Britton's trial drew an even more emotionally charged pool, with more than 90 of 120 people reporting strong feelings. His defense questioned how the home was handled by troopers, noting that officers were not wearing gloves in a house later deemed a crime scene. Britton testified that he waited more than a day to report Nola's death because he feared Randall would harm their unborn child. After about five hours of deliberation, jurors found him guilty on three of ten counts, including accessory after the fact to murder. They found him not guilty on all of the abuse charges. He faces up to 16 years in prison and is expected to be sentenced in December 2026.

Questions That Remain

The two verdicts draw a legal line between knowing about a death and being proven to have caused it. They do not settle a harder question. Nola's custody case was filed in Indiana while she lived in Maryland. At Randall's trial, Nola's father testified that there were months when he was not allowed to see his daughter, and that when he finally did, he noticed scars on her body.

At a press conference, officials could not say whether authorities had ever been called to the home before, or who held custody. Cecil County's executive pledged continued support for social services and child advocacy groups. But no budget can close the gap between two states' records. A whole region mobilized for a kidnapping that never happened. Why did the long-running signs of harm to Nola not trigger the same urgency?
