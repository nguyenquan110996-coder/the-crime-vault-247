Kamille "Cupcake" McKinney: How a Car Put Justice on Hold

The Crime Vault 247 · November 23, 2026 · Case: Kamille McKinney

Who took the life of a 3-year-old girl, and why has no jury answered that question more than seven years later?

On the night of October 12, 2019, 3-year-old Kamille McKinney, whom everyone in her Birmingham, Alabama neighborhood called "Cupcake," was playing at a birthday party at the Tom Brown Village housing complex. Her mother, April Thomas, stepped inside for about three minutes. When she came back out, Cupcake was gone.

Three Minutes and a City Search

An emergency alert described a man and a woman in a dark SUV, possibly an older Toyota. The vehicle was later identified as a Toyota Sequoia. For ten days, people across Birmingham searched. Cupcake's grandmother pleaded publicly: "Let the baby go. She wants her family."

Investigators said security video showed Cupcake outside an apartment complex with another toddler, and that a man appeared to talk to the children before they followed him. Her three-year-old friend told adults that Cupcake "went to get candy." According to testimony at trial, a man and a woman in a Toyota had also offered candy to a 14-year-old girl near a nearby school the same day.

Two Suspects and a Release

Police identified two people: Patrick Stallworth and Derick Brown. While the city was still searching, Stallworth was released from jail on a $500,000 bond. He had been held on unrelated charges, and at that point nothing in the file named Cupcake. Brown was also arrested on other charges at first.

A gas station camera had recorded the Toyota Sequoia on the day Cupcake disappeared. Investigators then traced trash from the neighborhood to a local landfill, where Cupcake's body was found about ten days after she vanished. An FBI agent later testified that two hours later, she might never have been found.

The Evidence

According to testimony, FBI phone records placed Stallworth at the scene of the kidnapping. A DNA analysis found the DNA of Stallworth, Brown and Cupcake on a plastic mattress cover, and tests showed methamphetamine and the sleep drug trazodone in the child's blood. The autopsy could not establish every detail because of the condition of her remains.

The accounts of the two defendants conflicted. Each pointed to the other. At a preliminary hearing, Judge Clyde Jones found that Cupcake was abducted, forced to take drugs, and killed by one or both of them, and sent Brown's capital murder charge to a grand jury. That finding does not say which of them caused her death. A text sent from a jail tablet on Stallworth's account read, in part, "I'm sorry I did this. It was an accident." The defense asked about the possibility that other inmates used the tablet.

Why Federal Court Came First

Federal prosecutors charged kidnapping resulting in death. Under that law, a jury does not have to decide which defendant caused the death. One of the grounds cited for federal jurisdiction was the use of the Toyota Sequoia.

A jury convicted Stallworth on October 7, 2022, after about 62 minutes. Another jury convicted Brown on November 17, 2022, after about an hour and 15 minutes. On March 3, 2023, both were sentenced to life without parole. Brown's federal sentence is to run consecutively to a state sentence that has not been imposed.

The Murder Trial That Never Came

Alabama is still pursuing capital murder charges against both and is seeking the death penalty. Neither has been convicted on those state charges. The state trial was pushed from March to September 2024, and since January 2025, every court date has been on hold while the federal appeal plays out.

That appeal is now at the U.S. Supreme Court. Brown's petition, No. 25-1342, does not claim she is innocent. It asks whether a federal court can take a kidnapping case that happened inside one state just because a car was used. The Eleventh Circuit said yes, holding that a car is an instrumentality of interstate commerce. Brown's side argues that this gives Congress nearly unlimited power over anything involving a car and notes that the Tenth Circuit has read the law more narrowly. The Cato Institute supports her. The government argues that cars travel on interstate highways and review is unnecessary.

The petition was listed for the justices' private conferences on October 16, November 6 and November 13, 2026. As of the date of this article, the public docket does not show a decision to grant or deny review.

What Could Happen Next

If the Court denies the petition, Brown's federal appeal ends and Alabama could set a new trial date. If it takes the case, briefing and argument could keep the state trial waiting. If Brown wins, her federal conviction could be thrown out, but the Alabama murder charges would still stand.

Whatever the justices decide, the question in the petition is about a car, not about who killed Cupcake. That question is still waiting in a Birmingham courtroom. In Birmingham, her family and neighbors remember a smile, the color purple, and one phrase: "Cupcake looks pretty."
